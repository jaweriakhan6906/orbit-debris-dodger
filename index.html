What am I actually looking at?

This shows the real bands where satellites and tracked debris orbit Earth — Low Earth Orbit (~400–2,000km), Medium Earth Orbit (~20,000km, GPS-type satellites), and Geostationary Orbit (~35,786km).

The app first tries to load real, current orbital elements from the public NORAD/CelesTrak catalog. If that's blocked by your network, it falls back to a realistically-generated dataset — the badge in the top bar always tells you honestly which one you're seeing.

Orbits are simplified circular approximations from each object's altitude and inclination — accurate enough about crowding and near-misses, but not a full physics-grade propagator.

A few close-approach scenarios are deliberately scripted to occur within a couple of minutes of simulated time, so the "watch two objects nearly collide" moment doesn't depend on getting lucky with random real data live.

The density heatmap, "launch a satellite" tool, and mission-control command bar are illustrative interactive features built on top of this same simplified model — fun and genuinely informative about orbital crowding, not mission-planning-grade tools.

Zoom out far enough and the view expands to a schematic solar system (planets and orbit spacing compressed for visibility, clearly not to scale), and further still to a handful of real nearby stars — positioned for clarity, with their actual distances in light-years shown on click.
